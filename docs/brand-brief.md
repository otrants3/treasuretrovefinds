# Treasure Trove Finds: what this actually is

Written 2026-08-11 after A.J. rejected six design directions. His diagnosis, and
he is right: the name means nothing on the page, his parents are barely on it,
and nothing about it stands out. This document is the missing piece. Design
decisions come from here, not from taste.

## The name, taken seriously

A treasure trove is a hoard of valuable things that somebody found. The word
promises two things: **abundance** and **discovery**.

Every version of this site so far has shown six photos in a tidy grid. That is
scarcity and order, the exact opposite of what the name promises, and it is also
a lie about the booth, which is packed floor to ceiling. If the site is honest,
it is FULL. You should feel like you could dig through it.

So: **density is not decoration here, it is the brand.**

## What makes Bill different

Not "a dealer with old things." Every booth in that mall has old things.

Bill has loved history since he was a kid on Long Island. He still does the
homework: the year, the maker, the mechanism, what it sold for at auction. He
learns a thing by studying it, in his own words, and then he can tell you.
He writes what he learned on the tag, by hand, and ties it to the object.

Other vendors write a price. Bill writes the record.

And the cases are open. Ask for anything and it comes out. In a mall where
everything is locked, that is a real policy and it is the physical version of
the same trait: he wants you to look closely.

## What makes Lisa different

**We still do not know, and that is now the biggest hole in this project.**
Everything on the site leans on Bill because Bill is the only one on tape. Half
this business is missing from its own website. Nothing is invented in the
meantime. Getting Lisa in her own words is the top content blocker.

## The thesis, one line

**Everything here was found by someone who wanted to know about it.**

Plainer, for a stranger who landed from Google: this is Booth 18 at Paradise
Antique Mall in Las Vegas. It is full of old things. The guy who found them can
tell you what each one is.

## What the site must do in the first five seconds

A first-time visitor who has never heard of them must instantly get:
1. What this is (an antique booth, a real physical place, in Las Vegas)
2. That it is full of interesting stuff (shown, not claimed)
3. That a real person with real knowledge is behind it
4. Where it is and how to reach him

If any of those needs a scroll or a thought, the design failed.

## The signature: the site IS the trove

One organizing idea, not a paint job:

**A dense wall of real objects. Tap any of them and you get what Bill knows.**

- The wall uses every real photo we have, not a curated six. More photos make it
  better, which turns Bill's weekly phone photos into the engine of the site.
- The payoff for a tap is the RECORD: what it is, who made it, when, what it
  costs, what Bill found out. The differentiator becomes the interaction.
- It reads as a trove on a phone (a scrolling mosaic) and as a wall on a desktop.
- No other antique site does this. They all do tidy product grids or beige lists.

## Palette, sampled from the booth itself

Taken from the real photos with ffmpeg, not chosen from taste. The room is warm
oat pegboard, oak cases, deep brown shadow, and the reds and greens that come off
the toys under fluorescent light.

```
--oat      #F4EEE2   ground, the pegboard and paper
--paper    #FCF8F0   cards and reading surfaces
--ink      #23201C   text, warm near-black (never pure black)
--oak      #A78862   frames, rules, the cases
--tin      #BE3A2B   the toys' red: prices, calls to action, one thing at a time
--sage     #4E6B58   deep green from the case lighting, rare second accent
--slate    #7C96A5   the cool fluorescent light, small labels only
```

Rules: oat is the ground. Tin red is for prices and actions only, never
decoration. Oak does structure. Never pure white, never pure black.

## Hard nos, learned from A.J.'s reactions

- No 1930s primary red/yellow/blue (Toy Box was closest in energy, wrong in color)
- No hero photo of Bill facing away from the camera. Until a real portrait
  arrives, the hero leads with the name and the objects, not a bad photo of him.
- No portrait video sitting beside a square photo. Portrait video gets a portrait
  frame, deliberately composed.
- No single-column-at-all-widths. Desktop must be art directed.
- Nothing "salesy," nothing that looks dated.
- No tidy six-photo grid standing in for a full booth.

## Content blockers, in priority order

1. **Lisa, in her own words.** Half the business is missing.
2. **A real portrait of Bill facing the camera.** Gates the hero.
3. More object photos. The wall is hungry, and it is the site's engine.
4. Bill's read of the story copy before it ships.
5. Confirm the mall's exact name, the booth's daily hours, the 2025 opening.
